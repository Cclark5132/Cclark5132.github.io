Compression is the hard load case for thin structures. A part in tension fails when the material fails. A part in compression can fail long before that, by suddenly bowing sideways: **buckling**. Buckling is a stability failure. It depends on **stiffness** ($E$ and geometry), not on strength, which is why a margin on von Mises stress tells you nothing about it. This week covers the two building blocks of stiffened-shell buckling: **columns** (stringers between frames) and **flat plates** (skin between stringers).

## 1. Euler column buckling

A perfectly straight, elastic column under an axial load $P$ stays straight until $P$ reaches a critical value. At that load, a slightly bent shape is also in equilibrium, and any tiny disturbance makes the column bow out:

<Formula
  label="Euler critical load"
  tex="P_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 E I}{(K L)^2}"
  vars="P_{cr} | critical (buckling) load | N; E | Young's modulus | MPa; I | smallest second moment of area of the cross-section | mm⁴; K | effective length factor for the end conditions | —; L | unsupported length of the column | mm"
/>

Three things to notice:

- **Use the smallest $I$.** A column buckles about its weakest axis. For a flat bar that is the thin direction.
- **Length is squared.** Doubling $L$ cuts $P_{cr}$ to $1/2^2 = 1/4$. Halving the bay length (adding a frame in the middle) multiplies it by 4.
- **Strength doesn't appear.** A high-strength steel column of the same shape buckles at the same load as a mild steel one, because $E$ is nearly the same.

### End conditions and effective length

The product $KL$ is the **effective length**: the length of the equivalent pinned-pinned column, or the distance between inflection points of the buckled shape.

| End conditions | Theoretical $K$ |
|---|---|
| Pinned–pinned | 1.0 |
| Fixed–fixed | 0.5 |
| Fixed–pinned | 0.7 (0.699) |
| Fixed–free (flagpole) | 2.0 |

Real "fixed" ends are never perfectly rigid, so design codes often recommend slightly larger values (for example 0.65 instead of 0.5 for fixed–fixed). Treat the theoretical values as best-case.

## 2. Critical stress and slenderness

Dividing $P_{cr}$ by the area $A$ gives a critical **stress**. Writing $I = A r^2$ makes the geometry collapse into one number:

<Formula
  label="Radius of gyration"
  tex="r = \sqrt{\frac{I}{A}}"
  vars="r | radius of gyration of the cross-section (about the weak axis) | mm; I | second moment of area (weak axis) | mm⁴; A | cross-sectional area | mm²"
/>

<Formula
  label="Euler critical stress"
  tex="\sigma_{cr} = \frac{P_{cr}}{A} = \frac{\pi^2 E}{(K L / r)^2}"
  vars="\sigma_{cr} | critical buckling stress | MPa; E | Young's modulus | MPa; K L / r | slenderness ratio (effective length over radius of gyration) | —"
/>

The **slenderness ratio** $KL/r$ says everything about a column's geometry. Two columns with different shapes but the same $KL/r$ (and the same material) buckle at the same stress. For a thin-walled round tube, $r \approx R_\text{mean}/\sqrt{2}$, which is why tubes make efficient columns: they put the material far from the axis.

## 3. Short columns: the Johnson parabola

Euler's formula has a flaw at low slenderness: as $KL/r \to 0$, $\sigma_{cr} \to \infty$. Real short columns can't carry more than the material allows. They fail by a mix of yielding and buckling at stresses below both the Euler value and $\sigma_y$. The **Johnson parabola** is the classic empirical fix:

<Formula
  label="Johnson parabola"
  tex="\sigma_{cr} = \sigma_y - \frac{\sigma_y^2}{4\pi^2 E}\left(\frac{K L}{r}\right)^2"
  vars="\sigma_{cr} | critical stress for an intermediate or short column | MPa; \sigma_y | compressive yield strength | MPa; E | Young's modulus | MPa; K L / r | slenderness ratio | —"
/>

The parabola starts at $\sigma_y$ for a zero-length column and meets the Euler curve tangentially at $\sigma_{cr} = \sigma_y/2$. That meeting point is the **transition slenderness**:

<Formula
  label="Transition slenderness ratio"
  tex="\left(\frac{K L}{r}\right)_c = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi^2 E}{\sigma_y}}"
  vars="(K L / r)_c | slenderness where Johnson and Euler meet, at a stress of half of yield | —; E | Young's modulus | MPa; \sigma_y | compressive yield strength | MPa"
/>

**The rule:** compute $KL/r$ first. If it is **greater** than $(KL/r)_c$, the column is long and Euler applies. If it is **less**, use Johnson. Using Euler on a short column is unconservative, sometimes wildly so.

<Callout kind="note" title="Stainless steels and the tangent modulus">
Johnson assumes a material with a fairly sharp yield point. Austenitic stainless steels like 304L have a gradual, rounded stress-strain curve, so the stiffness starts dropping well below the 0.2% yield. For real work, intermediate columns of these materials are better handled with the **tangent-modulus** method (replace $E$ in Euler with the local slope $E_t$ of the stress-strain curve). Johnson is the right tool for learning and quick estimates.
</Callout>

<Example title="Long stainless tube column (Euler)">

A stainless tube is 50 mm OD with a 2 mm wall (ID 46 mm), $L = 2{,}000$ mm, pinned at both ends. Use $E = 193{,}000$ MPa and a practice compressive yield of $\sigma_y = 300$ MPa. Find the buckling load.

**Step 1.** Section properties:

$$
A = \frac{\pi}{4}\left(50^2 - 46^2\right) = \frac{\pi}{4}(384) = 301.6 \text{ mm}^2
$$

$$
I = \frac{\pi}{64}\left(50^4 - 46^4\right) = \frac{\pi}{64}(1{,}772{,}544) = 87{,}010 \text{ mm}^4
$$

$$
r = \sqrt{\frac{87{,}010}{301.6}} = \sqrt{288.5} = 16.99 \text{ mm}
$$

**Step 2.** Slenderness, with $K = 1.0$:

$$
\frac{K L}{r} = \frac{1.0 \times 2{,}000}{16.99} = 117.75
$$

**Step 3.** Transition slenderness:

$$
\left(\frac{K L}{r}\right)_c = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi^2 (193{,}000)}{300}} = \sqrt{12{,}699} = 112.7
$$

Since $117.75 > 112.7$, the column is long and **Euler applies**.

**Step 4.** Critical stress and load:

$$
\sigma_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 (193{,}000)}{117.75^2} = \frac{1{,}904{,}834}{13{,}865} = 137.4 \text{ MPa}
$$

$$
P_{cr} = \sigma_{cr} A = 137.4 \times 301.6 = 41{,}440 \text{ N} \approx 41.4 \text{ kN}
$$

**Check** with the load form: $P_{cr} = \pi^2 (193{,}000)(87{,}010)/2{,}000^2 = 41.4$ kN.

**Result:** $P_{cr} \approx 41.4$ kN at $\sigma_{cr} = 137$ MPa, less than half the yield strength. Buckling, not yielding, sets this column's capacity.

</Example>

<Example title="Same tube, fixed–fixed (Johnson)">

Now clamp both ends of the same 2,000 mm tube, so $K = 0.5$.

**Step 1.** Slenderness:

$$
\frac{K L}{r} = \frac{0.5 \times 2{,}000}{16.99} = 58.87
$$

Since $58.87 < 112.7$, the column is intermediate and **Johnson applies**.

**Step 2.** What Euler would (wrongly) predict:

$$
\sigma_\text{Euler} = \frac{\pi^2 (193{,}000)}{58.87^2} = 549.5 \text{ MPa}
$$

That is above $\sigma_y = 300$ MPa, which is impossible for this material.

**Step 3.** Johnson. The coefficient is:

$$
\frac{\sigma_y^2}{4\pi^2 E} = \frac{300^2}{4\pi^2 (193{,}000)} = \frac{90{,}000}{7{,}619{,}335} = 0.011812 \text{ MPa}
$$

$$
\sigma_{cr} = 300 - 0.011812 \times 58.87^2 = 300 - 0.011812 \times 3{,}466 = 300 - 40.9 = 259.1 \text{ MPa}
$$

**Step 4.** Critical load:

$$
P_{cr} = 259.1 \times 301.6 = 78{,}140 \text{ N} \approx 78.1 \text{ kN}
$$

**Result:** $P_{cr} \approx 78$ kN. Clamping the ends nearly doubled the capacity, not quadrupled it as Euler would suggest (166 kN), because the column is now short enough that yielding limits it.

</Example>

## 4. Flat-plate buckling

The skin between two stringers is a long, thin plate compressed along its length and supported along its long edges by the stringers. Plates buckle into a pattern of bulges, roughly square, with half-wavelength about equal to the width $b$. Their critical stress has the same form as a column's, but with $t/b$ in place of $r/L$:

<Formula
  label="Flat-plate buckling stress"
  tex="\sigma_{cr} = k\,\frac{\pi^2 E}{12\left(1 - \nu^2\right)}\left(\frac{t}{b}\right)^2"
  vars="\sigma_{cr} | critical compressive stress of the plate | MPa; k | buckling coefficient (edge supports and aspect ratio) | —; E | Young's modulus | MPa; \nu | Poisson's ratio | —; t | plate thickness | mm; b | loaded width: the distance between supported edges (stringer spacing) | mm"
/>

For a **long plate with both unloaded edges simply supported** in uniform compression, $k \approx 4.0$. Other common values: about 6.97 with both unloaded edges clamped, and about 0.43 with one unloaded edge free (like a stringer flange). Real stringer attachments fall between simply supported and clamped, and $k = 4$ is the usual conservative choice.

Note the scaling: $\sigma_{cr} \propto (t/b)^2$. Halving the stringer spacing raises skin buckling stress by 4×. This formula, like Euler's, is elastic. If $\sigma_{cr}$ comes out near or above the proportional limit, a plasticity correction is needed.

## 5. Post-buckling and effective width

Unlike a column, a plate supported on its edges does **not** collapse when it buckles. The center of the panel bows out and stops picking up more load, but the strips next to the stringers are held straight and keep carrying more stress. The skin is **post-buckled**, and it can carry load well above $\sigma_{cr}$.

The classic way to handle this is von Kármán's **effective width**: replace the real, non-uniform stress with two strips next to the supports, of total width $b_e$, carrying the edge stress $\sigma_\text{max}$, while the middle carries nothing:

<Formula
  label="Effective width (von Kármán approximation)"
  tex="b_e = b\,\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{cr}}{\sigma_\text{max}}}, \qquad P_\text{skin} = b_e\, t\, \sigma_\text{max}"
  vars="b_e | total effective width of skin carrying load (both edge strips together) | mm; b | actual panel width between stringers | mm; \sigma_{cr} | plate buckling stress | MPa; \sigma_\text{max} | stress at the supported edges (equal to the stringer stress), at or above the buckling stress | MPa; P_\text{skin} | load carried by the skin panel | N; t | skin thickness | mm"
/>

With $k = 4$ this is often written $b_e \approx 1.9\,t\sqrt{E/\sigma_\text{max}}$. The effective skin is added to the stringer's area when you check the stringer as a column, so stringer and skin are designed together.

<Callout kind="context" title="Why this matters for the booster">
A stiffened barrel under axial compression combines everything here: skin panels buckle between stringers (plate buckling), stringers buckle between frames (column buckling), and the whole shell can buckle (general instability, which frames resist). Booster compressive loads come from engine thrust pushing up from the bottom, the weight and inertia of the propellant above, aerodynamic bending near max-Q, and the loads applied when the tower catches the booster. Week 6 adds the thin-cylinder case, where shape imperfections matter much more than for columns.
</Callout>

<Example title="Skin panel between stringers">

A skin panel is $b = 200$ mm wide between stringers and $t = 2$ mm thick, a long plate with simply supported long edges. Use $E = 193{,}000$ MPa and $\nu = 0.29$. (a) Find the buckling stress. (b) If the stringers are loaded to $\sigma_\text{max} = 150$ MPa, how much load does the skin carry?

**Step 1.** The constant part:

$$
\frac{k\,\pi^2 E}{12\left(1 - \nu^2\right)} = \frac{4\pi^2 (193{,}000)}{12(1 - 0.0841)} = \frac{7{,}619{,}335}{10.9908} = 693{,}247 \text{ MPa}
$$

**Step 2.** Buckling stress with $t/b = 0.01$:

$$
\sigma_{cr} = 693{,}247 \times (0.01)^2 = 69.3 \text{ MPa}
$$

**Step 3.** Since $\sigma_\text{max} = 150 > 69.3$ MPa, the skin is buckled. Effective width:

$$
b_e = 200\sqrt{\frac{69.3}{150}} = 200 \times 0.680 = 136.0 \text{ mm}
$$

**Step 4.** Load carried by the skin:

$$
P_\text{skin} = 136.0 \times 2 \times 150 = 40{,}800 \text{ N} \approx 40.8 \text{ kN}
$$

**Result:** The panel buckles at 69 MPa but still carries 40.8 kN, compared with $200 \times 2 \times 150 = 60$ kN if it stayed flat. About 68% of the skin is effective. Whether skin buckling at limit load is acceptable is a design-requirements decision; many programs don't allow it on pressure-tight tank walls.

</Example>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake" title="Using Euler on a short column">
Always compute $KL/r$ and compare with $(KL/r)_c$ first. Below the transition, Euler overpredicts the capacity and can even give a stress above yield.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake" title="Using the wrong I or the wrong K">
Use the **minimum** $I$ (weak axis). Check the end conditions for each axis separately; a stringer can be close to fixed in one plane and close to pinned in the other.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake" title="Using b as the plate length">
In the plate formula, $b$ is the **loaded width**: the distance between the supported edges (stringer spacing), not the length between frames. For a long plate, the length hardly matters.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake" title="Treating buckling as a strength margin">
Buckling has its own allowable ($\sigma_{cr}$ or $P_{cr}$), not $\sigma_y$. A part can have a large von Mises margin and a negative buckling margin.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake" title="Forgetting the static step in Ansys">
Linear (eigenvalue) buckling needs a prestressed static solution first. The eigenvalue is a load multiplier on the applied loads; $P_{cr} = \lambda \times P_\text{applied}$.
</Callout>

## Practice problems

<Problem n={1}>
A pinned–pinned column (long enough for Euler) buckles at 40 kN. What is its buckling load if the length is doubled? If it is halved (and stays in the Euler range)?

<Solution>
$P_{cr} \propto 1/L^2$:

$$
P_{cr,2L} = \frac{40}{2^2} = 10 \text{ kN}, \qquad P_{cr,L/2} = 40 \times 2^2 = 160 \text{ kN}
$$

Halving the length may push the column below the transition slenderness, in which case Johnson gives a lower value. Always recheck $KL/r$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={2}>
The tube from Worked Example 1 (50 × 2 mm, $r = 16.99$ mm) is used as a 1,000 mm flagpole: fixed at the base, free at the top. Find $P_{cr}$.

<Solution>
**Slenderness** with $K = 2.0$:

$$
\frac{K L}{r} = \frac{2.0 \times 1{,}000}{16.99} = 117.75 > 112.7 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{Euler}
$$

**Load:**

$$
\sigma_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 (193{,}000)}{117.75^2} = 137.4 \text{ MPa}, \qquad P_{cr} = 137.4 \times 301.6 = 41.4 \text{ kN}
$$

Same answer as Example 1: a 1 m flagpole has the same effective length (2 m) as a 2 m pinned–pinned column.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={3}>
The same tube, $L = 2{,}000$ mm, is fixed at one end and pinned at the other ($K = 0.7$). Find $P_{cr}$.

<Solution>
**Slenderness:**

$$
\frac{K L}{r} = \frac{0.7 \times 2{,}000}{16.99} = 82.42 < 112.7 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{Johnson}
$$

**Stress:**

$$
\sigma_{cr} = 300 - 0.011812 \times 82.42^2 = 300 - 0.011812 \times 6{,}793 = 300 - 80.2 = 219.8 \text{ MPa}
$$

**Load:**

$$
P_{cr} = 219.8 \times 301.6 = 66{,}290 \text{ N} \approx 66.3 \text{ kN}
$$

(Euler would have given $\pi^2 (193{,}000)/82.42^2 = 280$ MPa, or 84.6 kN: 28% unconservative.)
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={4}>
A solid round rod, $d = 20$ mm, $L = 600$ mm, pinned–pinned. Same material ($E = 193{,}000$ MPa, practice $\sigma_y = 300$ MPa). Find $r$, $KL/r$ and $P_{cr}$.

<Solution>
**Section:** for a solid circle $r = d/4$:

$$
A = \frac{\pi (20)^2}{4} = 314.2 \text{ mm}^2, \qquad r = \frac{20}{4} = 5.0 \text{ mm}
$$

**Slenderness:**

$$
\frac{K L}{r} = \frac{600}{5.0} = 120 > 112.7 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{Euler}
$$

**Load:**

$$
\sigma_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 (193{,}000)}{120^2} = 132.3 \text{ MPa}, \qquad P_{cr} = 132.3 \times 314.2 = 41{,}570 \text{ N} \approx 41.6 \text{ kN}
$$

The rod buckles at about the same load as the 2 m tube in Example 1 while being less than a third as long. It has about the same area, but $r$ is only 5 mm vs. 17 mm.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={5}>
For the skin panel in Worked Example 3 ($b = 200$ mm, $k = 4$, same material), what thickness is needed so the skin doesn't buckle until 150 MPa?

<Solution>
**Solve the plate formula for $t$:**

$$
\sigma_{cr} = 693{,}247 \left(\frac{t}{b}\right)^2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad t = b\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{cr}}{693{,}247}}
$$

$$
t = 200\sqrt{\frac{150}{693{,}247}} = 200 \times 0.01471 = 2.94 \text{ mm}
$$

The alternative is to keep $t = 2$ mm and add stringers. Solving for $b$ with $t = 2$: $b = 2/0.01471 = 136$ mm. That's a mass trade between thicker skin and more stringers.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={6}>
The same 200 × 2 mm panel ($\sigma_{cr} = 69.3$ MPa) is loaded until the stringers reach $\sigma_\text{max} = 120$ MPa. Find the effective width and the skin load.

<Solution>
**Effective width:**

$$
b_e = 200\sqrt{\frac{69.3}{120}} = 200 \times 0.760 = 152.0 \text{ mm}
$$

**Skin load:**

$$

<Problem n={7}>
Why do you have to run a static analysis before a linear buckling analysis in Ansys, and how do you turn the result into a buckling load?

<Solution>
1. **Linear buckling is an eigenvalue problem** on the stiffness matrix plus the **stress-stiffness** (geometric) matrix. Compression lowers the effective stiffness; buckling is where it reaches zero.
2. The stress-stiffness matrix is built from the **stresses** in the structure, so Ansys needs a static solution under the applied loads first.
3. The result is a **load multiplier** $\lambda$ for each mode: $P_{cr} = \lambda \times P_\text{applied}$. If you apply 1 kN and get $\lambda = 41.4$, the buckling load is 41.4 kN.
4. Pressure or other loads that should **not** be scaled (for example, tank pressure in a prestressed buckling run) need special setup so they're held constant while the axial load is scaled.
</Solution>
</Problem>
