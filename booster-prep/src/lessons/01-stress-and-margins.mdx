Every structures task ends with the same question: **does the part have positive margin for every load case?** This lesson builds the vocabulary for answering it: loads on a stage, stress and strain, combining stresses into one number, and turning that number into a margin.

## 1. Loads on a vertical rocket stage

Draw the free body diagram before you calculate anything. For a stage flying vertically, four kinds of load act on it:

- **Thrust** $T$ from the engines, pushing up at the thrust structure.
- **Weight** $W = mg$ acting down through the center of gravity.
- **Inertia.** When the stage accelerates upward at $a$, every kilogram of structure and propellant "pushes back" with $m\,a$. It is convenient to combine gravity and acceleration into a **load factor** $n$ (in g's).
- **Aerodynamic loads.** Drag acts along the axis. A side gust or angle of attack makes a normal force $N$ that bends the vehicle, worst near max-Q (maximum dynamic pressure).

Cut the stage at any station and keep the part above the cut. The wall at the cut must carry an **axial force** $P$ that accelerates everything above it, and a **bending moment** $M$ from the aerodynamic normal force. For mass $m_\text{above}$ above the cut:

<Formula
  label="Axial compression at a station"
  tex="P = m_\text{above}\, n\, g, \qquad n = \frac{a + g}{g}"
  vars="P | axial compressive force carried by the wall at the cut | N; m_\text{above} | mass of everything above the cut | kg; n | axial load factor | g's; a | upward acceleration of the vehicle | m/s²; g | gravitational acceleration, 9.81 | m/s²"
/>

The thin wall of radius $R$ and thickness $t$ turns these into stress. Axial force spreads around the circumference; bending adds tension on one side and compression on the other:

<Formula
  label="Wall stress from axial force and bending"
  tex="\sigma_\text{axial} = \frac{P}{2\pi R t}, \qquad \sigma_\text{bending} = \frac{M}{\pi R^2 t}"
  vars="\sigma_\text{axial} | uniform axial stress in the wall | MPa; \sigma_\text{bending} | peak bending stress (thin-wall tube) | MPa; P | axial force | N; M | bending moment | N·mm; R | mean radius | mm; t | wall thickness | mm"
/>

## 1b. Running loads and equivalent axial load

Structures engineers rarely quote wall stress first. They talk in **running loads**: force per unit length of circumference, in N/mm or lbf/in. A running load doesn't depend on the wall thickness, so it is the natural way to pass loads from the loads group to the stress analyst, and to compare stations, joints and panels of different thickness. Divide by $t$ only at the end to get stress.

Around the circumference of a thin cylinder, axial force spreads evenly, while bending adds running load on one side and subtracts it on the other:

<Formula
  label="Axial running load from axial force and bending"
  tex="N_x = \frac{P}{2\pi R} \pm \frac{M}{\pi R^2}, \qquad \sigma = \frac{N_x}{t}"
  vars="N_x | axial running load (force per unit length of circumference) | N/mm; P | axial force | N; M | bending moment | N·mm; R | mean radius | mm; t | wall thickness | mm; \sigma | wall stress | MPa"
/>

The peak running load is on the side where bending adds to the axial force. Buckling checks usually want one number: the **equivalent axial load**, the uniform axial force that gives the same peak running load as $P$ and $M$ together. Set $P_\text{eq}/(2\pi R)$ equal to the peak:

$$
\frac{P_\text{eq}}{2\pi R} = \frac{P}{2\pi R} + \frac{M}{\pi R^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad P_\text{eq} = P + \frac{2M}{R}
$$

<Formula
  label="Equivalent axial load"
  tex="P_\text{eq} = P + \frac{2M}{R}"
  vars="P_\text{eq} | uniform axial load that gives the same peak running load as P plus M | N; P | axial force | N; M | bending moment | N·mm; R | mean radius | mm"
/>

<Example title="Running load and equivalent axial load at a station">

A 9 m barrel ($R = 4500$ mm) carries $P = 30$ MN of axial compression and a bending moment of $M = 20$ MN·m (both practice values). Find the axial and bending running loads, the peak running load and $P_\text{eq}$.

**Step 1.** Convert the moment to N·mm:

$$
M = 20 \text{ MN·m} = 20 \times 10^6 \text{ N} \times 10^3 \text{ mm} = 20 \times 10^9 \text{ N·mm}
$$

**Step 2.** Axial part:

$$
\frac{P}{2\pi R} = \frac{30 \times 10^6}{2\pi \times 4500} = \frac{30 \times 10^6}{28{,}274} = 1061.0 \text{ N/mm}
$$

**Step 3.** Bending part:

$$
\frac{M}{\pi R^2} = \frac{20 \times 10^9}{\pi \times 4500^2} = \frac{20 \times 10^9}{6.362 \times 10^7} = 314.4 \text{ N/mm}
$$

**Step 4.** Peak running load (bending adds on the compression side):

$$
N_{x,\text{peak}} = 1061.0 + 314.4 = 1375.4 \text{ N/mm}
$$

**Step 5.** Equivalent axial load, with $R = 4.5$ m and $M$ in MN·m so the result is in MN:

$$
P_\text{eq} = P + \frac{2M}{R} = 30 + \frac{2 \times 20}{4.5} = 30 + 8.89 = 38.89 \text{ MN}
$$

**Check:** spreading $P_\text{eq}$ evenly gives the same peak:

$$
\frac{38.89 \times 10^6}{2\pi \times 4500} = 1375.4 \text{ N/mm} \;\checkmark
$$

**Result:** the bending moment adds about 30% to the compression the wall has to carry at the worst point. A 4 mm wall would see $1375.4 / 4 = 344$ MPa there.

</Example>

## 2. Stress, strain and Hooke's law

Stress is force per area, $\sigma = F/A$. Strain is stretch per length, $\varepsilon = \Delta L / L$ (dimensionless). In the elastic range they are proportional:

<Formula
  label="Hooke's law (uniaxial)"
  tex="\sigma = E\,\varepsilon"
  vars="\sigma | normal stress | MPa; E | Young's modulus (193 GPa = 193,000 MPa for 304L) | MPa; \varepsilon | normal strain | mm/mm"
/>

A tank wall is loaded in two directions at once (hoop and axial). Poisson's ratio $\nu$ couples them: stretching in one direction shrinks the other.

<Formula
  label="Hooke's law (biaxial, plane stress)"
  tex="\varepsilon_x = \frac{\sigma_x - \nu\,\sigma_y}{E}, \qquad \varepsilon_y = \frac{\sigma_y - \nu\,\sigma_x}{E}"
  vars="\varepsilon_x, \varepsilon_y | normal strains in x and y | mm/mm; \sigma_x, \sigma_y | normal stresses in x and y | MPa; \nu | Poisson's ratio (about 0.29 for 304L) | —; E | Young's modulus | MPa"
/>

This is what a strain gauge measures: strain, not stress. You convert to stress with these equations.

## 3. Principal stresses and Mohr's circle

At any point, a 2D stress state $(\sigma_x, \sigma_y, \tau_{xy})$ can be rotated to the **principal directions**, where the shear stress is zero and the normal stresses are the largest and smallest possible:

<Formula
  label="Principal stresses (2D)"
  tex="\sigma_{1,2} = \frac{\sigma_x + \sigma_y}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2}"
  vars="\sigma_1, \sigma_2 | maximum and minimum principal stresses | MPa; \sigma_x, \sigma_y | normal stresses on the x and y faces | MPa; \tau_{xy} | shear stress | MPa"
/>

Mohr's circle draws this. Its center is at $C = (\sigma_x + \sigma_y)/2$ and its radius is $R_M = \sqrt{((\sigma_x - \sigma_y)/2)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2}$. The circle crosses the horizontal axis at $\sigma_1 = C + R_M$ and $\sigma_2 = C - R_M$, and the radius is the maximum in-plane shear stress.

## 4. Von Mises equivalent stress

A ductile metal yields when its distortion energy reaches a limit. Von Mises combines a multi-axial stress state into one number you can compare with the uniaxial yield strength:

<Formula
  label="Von Mises (2D, principal stresses)"
  tex="\sigma_{vm} = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 - \sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2^2}"
  vars="\sigma_{vm} | von Mises equivalent stress | MPa; \sigma_1, \sigma_2 | in-plane principal stresses | MPa"
/>

<Formula
  label="Von Mises (2D, any axes)"
  tex="\sigma_{vm} = \sqrt{\sigma_x^2 - \sigma_x\sigma_y + \sigma_y^2 + 3\tau_{xy}^2}"
  vars="\sigma_x, \sigma_y | normal stresses | MPa; \tau_{xy} | shear stress | MPa"
/>

Both forms give the same answer, so you can skip finding principal stresses when you only need $\sigma_{vm}$.

## 5. Factor of safety and margin of safety

The **factor of safety** (FS) is a required multiplier on the load, set by the program's requirements (for example 1.25 on yield and 1.4 on ultimate). The **margin of safety** (MS) says how much capability is left over *after* the factor of safety is applied:

<Formula
  label="Margin of safety"
  tex="MS = \frac{\sigma_\text{allowable}}{FS \times \sigma_\text{applied}} - 1"
  vars="MS | margin of safety (pass when MS ≥ 0) | —; \sigma_\text{allowable} | material allowable (yield or ultimate) | MPa; FS | required factor of safety | —; \sigma_\text{applied} | applied (limit) stress | MPa"
/>

- $MS = 0$: exactly meets the requirement.
- $MS = +0.25$: could take 25% more load and still meet the requirement.
- $MS < 0$: does **not** meet the requirement. It doesn't mean the part will break, but it can't be signed off.

Aerospace reports use margins because zero is always the pass line, whichever factor of safety or failure mode applies. You can line up yield, ultimate, buckling and joint margins in one table and the smallest one is the one that matters.

### Limit, ultimate, proof, MEOP: the vocabulary

These words appear in every stress report and requirements document. The values below are the practice values this site uses, not any program's real criteria.

- **Limit load:** the maximum load expected in service. Loads groups deliver limit loads.
- **Ultimate load:** limit load × the ultimate factor of safety. The structure must carry ultimate load without failing.
- **Yield and ultimate factors of safety:** separate factors for the two checks, for example 1.25 on yield (no permanent set at limit × 1.25) and 1.4 on ultimate (no rupture at limit × 1.4). Pair each with its own allowable.
- **Buckling factor of safety:** the factor on the destabilizing load in a stability check, 1.4 in the capstone. It never multiplies a relieving load such as pressure end load; that is taken at its minimum guaranteed value, unfactored.
- **MEOP (maximum expected operating pressure):** the highest pressure a tank or line is expected to see in operation, including head and regulator tolerances.
- **Proof factor:** proof pressure ÷ MEOP. A proof test pressurizes real hardware to MEOP × proof factor (1.25 in the capstone) to screen out flaws. The check is usually no yield (or no detrimental deformation) at proof.
- **Burst factor:** burst (design ultimate) pressure ÷ MEOP. The tank must not rupture below MEOP × burst factor, which is higher than the proof factor.
- **Fitting factor:** an extra factor that many programs apply to fittings, lugs and joints, where load paths and stress concentrations are hard to predict. Learn it as a concept; the program's criteria set the number.

**How margins are written in a stress report:**

- Round margins **down** to 2 decimals: $+0.0749$ is reported as $+0.07$, never rounded up.
- Write a very large margin as **MS = +HIGH** instead of a big number nobody needs.
- Always name the **governing case**: the load case, the failure mode and the location that give the minimum margin.

<Example title="Margin from the Week 1 self-check">

A part has $\sigma_\text{allowable} = 300$ MPa, $\sigma_\text{applied} = 200$ MPa and $FS = 1.4$.

**Step 1.** Apply the factor of safety to the applied stress:

$$
FS \times \sigma_\text{applied} = 1.4 \times 200 = 280 \text{ MPa}
$$

**Step 2.** Divide the allowable by that and subtract one:

$$
MS = \frac{300}{280} - 1 = 1.0714 - 1 = +0.071
$$

**Result:** $MS \approx +0.07$. The part passes with a 7% reserve.

</Example>

<Example title="Biaxial tank wall element">

A tank wall element sees $\sigma_x = 100$ MPa (axial) and $\sigma_y = 200$ MPa (hoop), with no shear. The practice yield allowable is 500 MPa with $FS = 1.25$. Find the yield margin.

**Step 1.** With no shear, these are already principal stresses: $\sigma_1 = 200$ MPa and $\sigma_2 = 100$ MPa.

**Step 2.** Von Mises:

$$
\sigma_{vm} = \sqrt{200^2 - (200)(100) + 100^2} = \sqrt{40{,}000 - 20{,}000 + 10{,}000} = \sqrt{30{,}000} = 173.2 \text{ MPa}
$$

**Step 3.** Margin on yield:

$$
MS = \frac{500}{1.25 \times 173.2} - 1 = \frac{500}{216.5} - 1 = +1.31
$$

**Result:** $MS = +1.31$. Notice that $\sigma_{vm}$ (173 MPa) is *less* than the hoop stress (200 MPa). Biaxial tension is slightly kinder than uniaxial tension under von Mises.

</Example>

<Example title="Principal stresses with shear">

A point has $\sigma_x = 120$ MPa, $\sigma_y = 40$ MPa and $\tau_{xy} = 30$ MPa (the Mohr's circle above).

**Step 1.** Center and radius:

$$
C = \frac{120 + 40}{2} = 80 \text{ MPa}, \qquad R_M = \sqrt{\left(\frac{120 - 40}{2}\right)^2 + 30^2} = \sqrt{1600 + 900} = 50 \text{ MPa}
$$

**Step 2.** Principal stresses:

$$
\sigma_1 = 80 + 50 = 130 \text{ MPa}, \qquad \sigma_2 = 80 - 50 = 30 \text{ MPa}
$$

**Step 3.** Von Mises, two ways:

$$
\sigma_{vm} = \sqrt{130^2 - (130)(30) + 30^2} = \sqrt{13{,}900} = 117.9 \text{ MPa}
$$

$$
\sigma_{vm} = \sqrt{120^2 - (120)(40) + 40^2 + 3(30)^2} = \sqrt{13{,}900} = 117.9 \text{ MPa}
$$

Both agree, as they must.

</Example>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake" title="Mixing running load and stress">
$N_x$ is in N/mm (force per length), stress is in N/mm² (MPa). Divide the running load by the thickness $t$ to get stress. Writing "1375 MPa" for a running load is a units error, not a scary stress.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake" title="Leaving out the factor of safety">
Writing $MS = \sigma_\text{allow}/\sigma_\text{applied} - 1$ gives a margin that's too high. The FS always multiplies the applied stress (or load) first.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake" title="Pairing the wrong FS with the wrong allowable">
Yield allowables pair with the yield FS (1.25 in the capstone), ultimate allowables with the ultimate FS (1.4). Check both and report the lower margin.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake" title="Using von Mises for buckling">
Von Mises predicts yielding. Buckling is a stability failure that can happen far below yield, so it gets its own margin with its own allowable (Weeks 5 and 6).
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake" title="Mixing units">
MPa is N/mm². If force is in N and lengths are in mm, stress comes out in MPa directly. Mixing meters and millimeters is the most common source of answers that are off by 1000.
</Callout>

## Practice problems

<Problem n={1}>
A bracket has a practice yield allowable of 480 MPa. The applied stress is 310 MPa and the yield FS is 1.25. What is the yield margin?

<Solution>
$$
MS = \frac{480}{1.25 \times 310} - 1 = \frac{480}{387.5} - 1 = +0.24
$$

The bracket passes with $MS = +0.24$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={2}>
The ultimate allowable is 700 MPa and the ultimate FS is 1.4. What is the largest applied stress that still gives $MS \geq 0$?

<Solution>
Set $MS = 0$ and solve for the applied stress:

$$
0 = \frac{700}{1.4\,\sigma_\text{applied}} - 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma_\text{applied} = \frac{700}{1.4} = 500 \text{ MPa}
$$
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={3}>
A point has $\sigma_x = -80$ MPa, $\sigma_y = 150$ MPa and $\tau_{xy} = 40$ MPa. Find the principal stresses and the von Mises stress.

<Solution>
**Center and radius:**

$$
C = \frac{-80 + 150}{2} = 35 \text{ MPa}, \qquad R_M = \sqrt{\left(\frac{-80 - 150}{2}\right)^2 + 40^2} = \sqrt{13{,}225 + 1{,}600} = 121.8 \text{ MPa}
$$

**Principal stresses:**

$$
\sigma_1 = 35 + 121.8 = 156.8 \text{ MPa}, \qquad \sigma_2 = 35 - 121.8 = -86.8 \text{ MPa}
$$

**Von Mises:**

$$
\sigma_{vm} = \sqrt{156.8^2 - (156.8)(-86.8) + (-86.8)^2} = \sqrt{24{,}586 + 13{,}610 + 7{,}534} = 213.8 \text{ MPa}
$$

Tension in one direction and compression in the other makes von Mises *larger* than either stress alone. Mixed-sign stress states are the harsh ones.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={4}>
A stage of total mass $1.0 \times 10^6$ kg has 20 MN of thrust at liftoff. Ignore drag. (a) What is its acceleration and load factor? (b) What axial force does the wall carry at a station with $4.0 \times 10^5$ kg above it?

<Solution>
**(a)** Net force divided by mass:

$$
a = \frac{T}{m} - g = \frac{20 \times 10^6}{1.0 \times 10^6} - 9.81 = 10.19 \text{ m/s}^2
$$

$$
n = \frac{a + g}{g} = \frac{10.19 + 9.81}{9.81} = 2.04
$$

**(b)** The wall must hold up and accelerate the mass above it:

$$
P = m_\text{above}\, n\, g = 4.0 \times 10^5 \times 2.04 \times 9.81 = 8.0 \times 10^6 \text{ N} = 8.0 \text{ MN}
$$

A shortcut: $P = m_\text{above} \times T/m$, which is $40\%$ of the thrust because $40\%$ of the mass is above the station.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={5}>
A strain gauge rosette on a 304L tank wall ($E = 193$ GPa, $\nu = 0.29$) reads $\varepsilon_x = 2.18 \times 10^{-4}$ (axial) and $\varepsilon_y = 8.86 \times 10^{-4}$ (hoop). The wall is in plane stress with no shear. Find $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_y$.

<Solution>
Invert the biaxial Hooke's law:

$$
\sigma_x = \frac{E}{1 - \nu^2}\left(\varepsilon_x + \nu\,\varepsilon_y\right), \qquad \sigma_y = \frac{E}{1 - \nu^2}\left(\varepsilon_y + \nu\,\varepsilon_x\right)
$$

$$
\frac{E}{1 - \nu^2} = \frac{193{,}000}{1 - 0.0841} = 210{,}722 \text{ MPa}
$$

$$
\sigma_x = 210{,}722 \times (2.18 + 0.29 \times 8.86) \times 10^{-4} = 210{,}722 \times 4.749 \times 10^{-4} \approx 100 \text{ MPa}
$$

$$
\sigma_y = 210{,}722 \times (8.86 + 0.29 \times 2.18) \times 10^{-4} = 210{,}722 \times 9.492 \times 10^{-4} \approx 200 \text{ MPa}
$$

These are the stresses from Worked Example 2. The hoop strain is about four times the axial strain even though the hoop stress is only twice the axial stress, because Poisson contraction from the hoop stress subtracts from the axial strain.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={6}>
Two load cases on the same weld land give yield margins of $+0.42$ and $-0.05$, and an ultimate margin of $+0.18$. What do you report, and what are your options?

<Solution>
Report the **minimum margin, $-0.05$ (yield)**, and the load case that drives it. The part does not meet the requirement. Options:

1. **Reduce the applied stress:** thicken the land, change the load path, or reduce the load.
2. **Raise the allowable:** a stronger material or processing condition, with real allowables data behind it.
3. **Refine the analysis:** remove conservatism (better load data, a more accurate stress model, test data), so the applied stress is calculated more accurately.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={7}>
A station has $R = 4500$ mm, $P = 12$ MN of axial compression and $M = 8$ MN·m of bending (practice values). Find the largest and smallest axial running loads around the circumference, and $P_\text{eq}$. Is any side in net tension?

<Solution>
**Axial and bending parts:**

$$
\frac{P}{2\pi R} = \frac{12 \times 10^6}{2\pi \times 4500} = 424.41 \text{ N/mm}, \qquad \frac{M}{\pi R^2} = \frac{8 \times 10^9}{\pi \times 4500^2} = 125.75 \text{ N/mm}
$$

**Largest and smallest:**

$$
N_{x,\max} = 424.41 + 125.75 = 550.2 \text{ N/mm}, \qquad N_{x,\min} = 424.41 - 125.75 = 298.7 \text{ N/mm}
$$

Both are compressive, so no side goes into tension; bending would need to exceed $PR/2 = 27$ MN·m for that.

**Equivalent axial load:**

$$
P_\text{eq} = 12 + \frac{2 \times 8}{4.5} = 12 + 3.56 = 15.56 \text{ MN}
$$
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem n={8}>
**US units.** A loads memo gives $P = 1{,}500{,}000$ lbf of compression and $M = 30{,}000{,}000$ in·lbf of bending at a station with $R = 177$ in. Find the peak running load in lbf/in and $P_\text{eq}$ in lbf, then give both in SI. Check your answer with the **SI / US toggle** on the Calculators page (running load card).

<Solution>
**Axial and bending parts:**

$$
\frac{P}{2\pi R} = \frac{1{,}500{,}000}{2\pi \times 177} = 1348.8 \text{ lbf/in}, \qquad \frac{M}{\pi R^2} = \frac{30{,}000{,}000}{\pi \times 177^2} = 304.8 \text{ lbf/in}
$$

**Peak running load:**

$$
N_{x,\text{peak}} = 1348.8 + 304.8 = 1653.6 \text{ lbf/in}
$$

**Equivalent axial load:**

$$
P_\text{eq} = 1{,}500{,}000 + \frac{2 \times 30{,}000{,}000}{177} = 1{,}500{,}000 + 338{,}983 = 1{,}838{,}983 \text{ lbf}
$$

**In SI** (1 lbf/in = 0.175127 N/mm, 1 lbf = 4.448 N):

$$
N_{x,\text{peak}} = 1653.6 \times 0.175127 = 289.6 \text{ N/mm}, \qquad P_\text{eq} = 1{,}838{,}983 \times 4.448 = 8.18 \text{ MN}
$$
</Solution>

<Problem n={9}>
The Example 1b station ($R = 4500$ mm, $P = 30$ MN limit, $M = 20$ MN·m limit, so $P_\text{eq} = 38.89$ MN) is pressurized to a guaranteed minimum of $p_\text{min} = 0.35$ MPa. The buckling FS is 1.4 (practice). With a 12 mm wall, SP-8007 gives $P_\text{allow} = 38.83$ MN (Lesson 6 method). Find the factored net running load and the buckling margin.

<Solution>
**Step 1.** Factor the destabilizing load only:

$$
FS \times P_\text{eq} = 1.4 \times 38.89 = 54.44 \text{ MN}
$$

**Step 2.** Pressure end load at the minimum pressure, unfactored:

$$
p_\text{min}\,\pi R^2 = 0.35 \times \pi \times 4500^2 = 22.27 \text{ MN}
$$

**Step 3.** Net load and net running load:

$$
P_\text{net} = 54.44 - 22.27 = 32.18 \text{ MN}, \qquad N_{x,\text{net}} = \frac{32.18 \times 10^6}{2\pi \times 4500} = 1138 \text{ N/mm}
$$

That is $1138 / 12 = 94.8$ MPa of factored net compression in the wall.

**Step 4.** Margin:

$$
MS = \frac{P_\text{allow}}{P_\text{net}} - 1 = \frac{38.83}{32.18} - 1 = +0.21
$$

The wall passes. Never multiply the 22.27 MN of relief by 1.4; that would make the margin look better than it is.
</Solution>
</Problem>
